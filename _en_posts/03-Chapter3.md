Strategic planning

If you think that after reading this chapter you will be able to plan election campaign, then you are too late. Planning must begin long before the election campaign begins.

Why do you need planning?

Each campaign has a limited amount of resources of time, money, people and talent. And if you can find additional money and attract the necessary people, then the resource of time cannot be replenished. You will need maximum concentration and effort to organize people in a short time and get the money needed to persuade the majority of voters to elect

your candidate.

Too often candidates, volunteers and candidate staff are literally consumed by the process, i.e.the team begins to simply react to current events. election campaign comes to chaos. The goal of election campaign planning is to create order out of chaos and ultimately avoid wasting money, time and effort.

Strategic planning

Strategic planning allows you to: - formulate the objectives of the campaign;


determine the size and significance of various types and groups of voters, their expectations and formulate an appropriate

IMAGE of the candidate;

set priorities and distribute efforts;

develop measures to promote the positive image of the candidate and level out his negative traits;



quickly manage resources;

determine the quantity and moment of attracting the necessary resources (financial, personnel, production); effectively allocate the candidate's time.

In past elections there were many examples when the consistency and purposefulness of a team’s actions made it possible to beat numerous and more lucrative opponents. For the one who knows where to go reaches his goal faster.

Setting goals

Setting and solving problems is a systematic process of assessing the received initial data for the district, allows you to arrange the polling stations of your district in priority order and develop optimal tactics for each specific precinct. Setting goals provides an opportunity to direct your election campaign's resources to work in the constituencies where they will have the greatest impact. This will allow you to make the best use of your strengths, discover the voters who are most likely to vote the way you want them to, and target your efforts to reach them. For example, demographic targeting is used to paint a picture of the "lifestyle" of different areas of a district and the types of voters who live there.

This type of analysis takes into account factors such as age, income, education, gender, etc.

Plot demographic information on a district map to identify general areas in which voters live who are similar in background and socioeconomic conditions.



Of course, we can’t take the demographic task too literally, but knowing the characteristics of voters makes it possible to get closer to their values ​​and concerns. In addition, in conjunction with election information, demographic data will make it possible to assess the “seriousness” of various groups’ intentions to participate in elections. And this will ultimately help avoid wasting time and effort in areas where there is little chance of increasing the number of “yours.”

votes."

Voter categories


The main goal of the election campaign is to attract the largest number of supporters to the candidate and his program and convince them to come to the polling stations and vote for your candidate. At the same time, you need to clearly understand that it makes no sense for you to waste your resources on those who will never, under any pretext, come to vote, and on those who definitely intend to vote for your rivals, i.e.against you. These two categories of voters should be identified and ignored. Your voter falls somewhere between these two extremes.

There is a widespread opinion that the overwhelming majority of ordinary citizens do not care about political problems. However, it is precisely this majority of voters, awakening from hibernation and becoming more active during election campaigns, that determines the composition of the electoral authorities, the further balance of power and the fate of the entire political life of the country for several years to come. Therefore, your election campaign strategy must ultimately answer the question: why should an ordinary voter vote for your candidate?

The task of collecting votes

## Before defining the problem, it is important to predict how many voters will show up at the polls. The easiest way to do this is to focus on voter turnout figures in previous elections for each polling station. At the same time, you should pay attention to factors that may affect voter turnout:

Are these elections held simultaneously with the presidential elections? What are the mood of the population and attitude towards these elections?the time of the elections and how this may affect voter turnout (beginning of seasonal gardening, vacation periods, weather conditions, etc.).



Voter outreach planning begins with figuring out how many votes a candidate needs to win. In this case, they rely on the maximum “planned” number of voter turnout. In other words, if the number of voters who turn out to vote is the same as last election, how many voters must vote for your candidate in order for him to win?

Each precinct is a separate election, and the task of collecting votes for each specific precinct must be determined and accomplished in order to win the overall vote. No one can be responsible for 2,000 votes, but everyone can compete for the 50 or 80 votes needed to win one precinct. Campaign goals and tactics

It shows exactly how you expect to receive the support of the majority of voters.

Goals are set for each specific group of voters that you identified when setting goals. They determine campaign tactics, time allocation for organizational and communication programs, use of finances, and priorities when planning the candidate's time.

Network schedule


Time management is an essential part of any campaign plan. This is a schedule of all your campaign tactics and the sequence of your actions. The schedule plays an important organizational role throughout the election campaign. It shows what, in what quantity and by what date you need to achieve your plan.

took place.

For example, in order for the March 8th holiday leaflet to end up in the hands of your voters, you need to: - distribute it to mailboxes on March 6-7;



no later than March 5, pick up copies of leaflets from the printing house, bring them to headquarters and sort them;

The production time for leaflets is 10 days, so on February 20 (the 21st is a Saturday and the printing house may not work) your original layout on films must be in the printing house; no later than February 13, you must submit the layout for color separation;

before February 9, you need to find a good designer and assign him the task of developing a flyer layout;

by February 7, the text of the leaflet must be ready and approved and a person responsible for carrying out this action must be appointed. Thus, in order to hold a specific event, the idea of ​​it must come to you at the end of January. It is also necessary to provide for not only technological deadlines, but also possible unforeseen failures and overlaps. To do this you will need a schedule.

Building a schedule

To create a schedule, start with Election Day and work backwards, using a blank desk-sized calendar chart. Start working with a pencil. Once you are done with the time allocation process, you can code different activities in different colors. After this, the plan will look like a finished painting.

You will know in advance how many human and financial resources will be needed to implement a particular program, what preparatory work needs to be done, and determine the critical deadlines for implementation. Use a timeline throughout your campaign to plan your activities month-by-month and week-by-week.

### Budget

**Budget** is the financial picture of the tactical plan. It reflects tactical priorities. Without an accurate link between plan and budget, your election campaign is doomed to failure. Many plans were revised after cash calculations were made.

To leave room for error, overestimate expenses and underestimate income. When creating a budget, “flip” the planning process: start with small parts. An accurate budget is the sum of its parts, so use estimates of individual expenses to get the overall numbers.

Expenses must be allocated over time periods. You must know exactly what amount you will need at what time. In addition, budget distribution allows you to control the income and expenditure of money on a monthly basis. You don't want to find yourself without money in the last month of the campaign.

- rent for headquarters premises, transportation costs; current expenses: purchase of stationery, maintenance of the headquarters, purchase of products, entertainment expenses (up to 10% of the budget amount);

- acquisition of necessary office equipment, equipment, consumables.

### Planning

When creating your strategy, the best place to start is by establishing initial assumptions: “Why do people vote.”These assumptions are created by analyzing research results and bringing together all available information. Already during familiarization with the information received, initial assumptions will become



take on the form of strategic hypotheses in your head: why people will vote for your candidate.

Roughly sketch out strategic hypotheses for your election campaign and for the campaigns of your “general rivals”. No rough plan is perfect. Again, decide whether you have all the necessary information to form sound hypotheses. Once you have a good outline, it is time to develop the final strategic plan.

Highlight the most important key points on which the main directions of further work will be formed and which will set the dynamics of election campaign.

The final version of the strategy should be described in seven to twelve clearly defined points. These talking points must clearly justify the fundamental reasons why your campaign will win.

The strategy and talking points must be written, clearly understood, and approved by the candidate, managers, and other key people in the campaign.

Here are a few possible strategy points that need to be reflected in the final strategy. What are the key reasons why voters will vote for your candidate?


What are the key reasons for rejecting the work of the main opponent?

What is the “core theme” that will be used throughout the election campaign?

What image do you want your candidate to portray? How will it be received?

What images will you paint for your opponents? How do you want your primary opponent to be perceived?

- Is there a philosophical (ideological) basis in your strategy?

How to prepare a plan

Now that all the elements of the strategic plan have become clear, let's return to the process of writing it.

The campaign plan must be effective. This requires unanimity in the development of the plan among all participants in the campaign. Often great plans fall through the cracks simply because the people who make the decisions in the campaign were not involved in creating the plan. Key campaigners should at least have a say when the rules of the game are created. In the end, they will be the ones playing on them. Typically, the planning team includes the candidate, kitchen cabinet members, and independent consultants.

From the very beginning, determine the “group leader” (this is not necessarily a candidate, more often it is the head of the election campaign). He must set time limits, distribute tasks, monitor everyone's work, and manage the overall work of the group. He must be sure that all members of the planning team have had their say.

To generate ideas for defining strategy and developing an image, the method of brainstorming followed by group discussion is most suitable. Brainstorming encourages the free flow of thoughts, without negative considerations. And compromises and support within the group lead to better results. One of the main planning conditions is that all proposals must be written down on paper.

The group must agree on the starting points, strategic hypotheses and definition of objects. When the framework has already been established and the main directions have been determined, one of the team members is given the specific task of writing a plan. The project is then discussed again and finally approved by the group members, especially the candidate. Your work begins the moment the plan is approved. You must form a headquarters, assign responsibilities, and determine how the goals of your plan will be accomplished.

Campaign stages

The plan must include the required number of voter contacts with positive information about the candidate. At the same time, you can develop your own contact programs for different areas of the district.

The following stages of the campaign are distinguished.

1. Zero (preparatory) stage.

Lasts until the official registration of candidates for deputies begins. It is advisable to use this time to collect information about public opinion and the appearance of “good deeds”, increasing the “background fame” of the candidate in the district through a series of special events, identifying the name (at the level: “Oh, this is so-and-so, I heard about him somewhere”).

2. Initial stage.

The beginning of the propaganda campaign, the first acquaintance with the candidate and his program. At the very beginning of the election campaign, the main emphasis is on identifying the candidate and distinguishing him from the general crowd. Therefore, its main identifier is positioned - the candidate’s last name and first name. This is done through individual actions (posters, articles, speeches, other information “noise”, scandals and rumors). The name must be firmly entrenched in the minds of voters, then selectivity of perception is activated; any information associated with a familiar name is noticed, extracted from the general context and remembered. It’s as if a separate box with the candidate’s last name appears in the brain, and all information relating to this last name is filtered and neatly placed in the box.

Sample Campaign Plan

Depending on the specifics of your district and available resources, you can plan seven direct contacts with voters (see example network schedule):

Stage 1 - posting posters-identifiers “I am a candidate” throughout the district on the main route lines; sending out the “Meet the Candidate” program to every mailbox;

Stage 2 - (continuation of distribution and related materials according to the headquarters schedule).

Stage 3 – “every door” campaigning. A candidate, campaigner or proxy visits the voter’s apartment, leaves information materials with him, collects information about the support, needs and demands of voters (“voter orders”);

Stage 4 - delivery of a newspaper with an article about the candidate to each mailbox;

Stage 5 - posting posters (leaflets) of the candidate;

Stage 6 - meetings with the candidate, phone calls;

Stage 7 - incentive leaflet, phone calls. This program is aimed at voters who have no particular bias and may support a candidate.

The schedule should be created in such a way that sufficient time is allocated for the preparation of activities.
